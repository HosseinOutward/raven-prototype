# Raven Manifesto

**Version:** 0.2  
**Purpose:** Product identity, philosophy, ethics, political stance, sociological assumptions, power-dynamic principles, technical direction, governance, business boundaries, and non-negotiable commitments for Raven.

---

## 0. Reading This Manifesto

This manifesto is not a branding document.

It is a constitution for product decisions.

It exists so that Raven does not become whatever the market, competitors, investors, enterprise customers, contributors, or passing trends pressure it to become. It should be used during product planning, architecture, UI/UX design, onboarding, governance, moderation design, business planning, and technical review.

Raven should be allowed to evolve. But it should not evolve by forgetting why it exists.

This document defines:

- What Raven is.
- What Raven believes about human communication.
- What Raven refuses to become.
- What kinds of power Raven creates.
- What responsibilities Raven has because of that power.
- What technical and business choices are morally compatible with Raven.
- What decisions must be rejected even if they are profitable, popular, or technically impressive.

Throughout this manifesto, **lean** means lightweight, bloat-free, streamlined, focused, resource-conscious, and intellectually disciplined. It does not refer to the lean startup methodology.

---

## 1. What Raven Is

Raven is a **lean, open-source, human-centric communication home** for people who want to talk, see, share, and gather with the lowest reasonable friction, without surrendering privacy, control, transparency, or dignity.

Raven is not merely a meeting app.  
Raven is not a Discord clone.  
Raven is not a Zoom alternative with different branding.  
Raven is not an enterprise surveillance layer.  
Raven is not a social feed.  
Raven is not a productivity cage.  
Raven is not an AI-generated communication machine.  
Raven is not a place where humans become metadata for someone else's dashboard.

Raven exists for real-time human communication: spontaneous calls, trusted spaces, small groups, meaningful reachability, and user-controlled presence.

Raven should feel like a **home**, not a dashboard.

A home is not merely a container. A home has boundaries, permissions, rhythms, rooms, warmth, familiarity, and trust. A home allows people to appear without performing, to leave without punishment, to return without ceremony, and to belong without being constantly measured.

Raven should feel calm, fast, trustworthy, and alive without becoming noisy.

The simplest product definition is:

> **Raven is a native, open-source communication home for trusted groups, built around privacy-respecting presence, low-friction voice/video, and user control.**

## 2. The Core Belief

Modern communication tools have become too opaque, too bloated, too centralized, too extractive, and too careless with human presence.

They often tell users:

> This is how communication works. Accept it or leave.

Raven rejects that.

Users deserve to understand, control, and shape the tools through which they speak, listen, appear, disappear, gather, and share themselves with others.

Raven believes that communication software should not treat people as:

- Data sources.
- Engagement units.
- Availability indicators.
- Productivity objects.
- Behavioral profiles.
- Monetizable attention streams.
- Resources to be routed, measured, optimized, and retained.

Raven treats users as **human beings in relation**.

Human communication is not just transmission. It is context, consent, vulnerability, timing, trust, silence, interruption, refusal, mood, presence, and responsibility.

A communication tool is therefore never neutral. It shapes how people become reachable, how they become visible, how they refuse, how they gather, how they perform, how they hide, how they trust, and how power moves between them.

Raven starts from this premise:

> **Every communication interface is also a social contract.**

## 3. The Human Problem Raven Solves

Raven is built against several failures of modern communication tools.

### 3.1 Opaque Communication

Users often do not know what happens to their calls, metadata, devices, recordings, usage patterns, or interactions.

They do not know what is collected, what is stored, what is inferred, what is visible to admins, what is visible to the company, what is processed by AI, what is retained, or what can later be used against them.

Raven should make communication legible without making users exposed.

Legibility must serve the user first, not the platform.

### 3.2 Loss of Control

Users are rarely allowed to control how they send, receive, process, route, or present their communication.

They are given defaults and told those defaults are simplicity.

But simplicity without agency becomes paternalism.

Raven should give meaningful control back to users, especially over:

- Audio behavior.
- Video behavior.
- Signal quality.
- Visibility.
- Privacy.
- Device state.
- Presence.
- Room access.
- Processing.
- Hosting.
- Appearance.
- Communication context.

Control must be designed carefully. Power should exist, but it should not be dumped onto the user as cognitive debt.

The Raven principle is:

> **Simple surface. Deep basement.**

### 3.3 Bloat

Communication tools often grow into ecosystems of unrelated features, feeds, dashboards, bots, AI assistants, productivity systems, engagement loops, admin machinery, and social-performance surfaces.

Bloat is not only a technical problem. It is a moral and sociological problem.

Bloat increases cognitive load.  
Bloat expands attack surface.  
Bloat weakens trust.  
Bloat creates maintenance debt.  
Bloat hides power.  
Bloat makes the product harder to understand.  
Bloat turns a tool into an environment users must survive.

Raven should remain lean: lightweight, focused, and bloat-resistant.

### 3.4 Social Pressure

Presence can create warmth, belonging, and spontaneity.

Presence can also become surveillance, obligation, judgment, and guilt.

Online indicators can become demands.  
Read receipts can become accusations.  
Activity history can become discipline.  
Availability can become entitlement.  
Visibility can become control.

Raven should make people meaningfully reachable without making them constantly exposed.

### 3.5 Corporate Mediation

Many communication tools are shaped by business models that depend on data extraction, lock-in, behavioral analytics, enterprise control, platform dependency, attention capture, or opaque infrastructure.

Raven should be commercially sustainable without becoming extractive.

The product must not confuse survival with obedience to the worst incentives of the market.

## 4. Raven’s Philosophical Foundation

Raven is not built from one philosophical school. It borrows from several traditions because communication is not merely technical. It is ethical, political, social, and existential.

### 4.1 Socratic Principle: Question the Given

Raven begins by questioning assumptions that most communication tools treat as obvious:

- Should users be constantly reachable?
- Should presence always be visible?
- Should admins know what people are doing?
- Should convenience override autonomy?
- Should privacy be traded for features?
- Should AI listen because it can summarize?
- Should enterprises define what communication software becomes?
- Should growth determine product truth?

Raven should preserve a Socratic distrust of inherited defaults.

If a feature exists only because competitors have it, Raven should interrogate it.

### 4.2 Aristotelian Principle: Know the Telos

The telos of Raven is not engagement, retention, virality, or enterprise adoption.

Raven’s telos is:

> **To help humans communicate in trusted real-time spaces with clarity, consent, control, and dignity.**

A good Raven feature helps the product fulfill that purpose.

A bad Raven feature may be popular, impressive, or profitable while still corrupting the product’s proper function.

Raven should cultivate virtues in its users:

- Trust.
- Honesty.
- Belonging.
- Responsibility.
- Self-restraint.
- Careful presence.
- Respect for boundaries.

Raven should avoid cultivating vices:

- Control obsession.
- Social anxiety.
- Performative availability.
- Surveillance hunger.
- Entitlement to others’ attention.
- Dependency on the platform.
- Indifference toward privacy.

### 4.3 Kantian Principle: Users Are Ends, Not Means

Raven must treat users as ends in themselves, not merely as means to growth, revenue, data, community size, or product validation.

This means:

- No selling user data.
- No dark patterns.
- No deceptive defaults.
- No hidden monitoring.
- No privacy as a paid privilege.
- No AI listening without consent.
- No enterprise features that reduce users to observable labor.

A user’s dignity does not become less important because they are using the free version, joining a hosted server, or participating in a community they do not own.

### 4.4 Heideggerian Principle: Do Not Reduce People to Standing Reserve

Modern technology often turns the world into a stockpile of resources to be optimized.

Communication platforms can do the same to people. They can turn users into availability, attention, sentiment, activity, content, productivity, graph edges, and monetizable behavioral traces.

Raven must resist this reduction.

A person in Raven is not primarily:

- Online.
- Busy.
- Idle.
- Speaking.
- Muted.
- Productive.
- Engaged.
- Retained.
- Monetizable.

A person in Raven is a being in context, with boundaries, intentions, moods, relationships, and the right not to be fully knowable.

### 4.5 Illichian Principle: Build Convivial Tools

Raven should be a convivial tool: a tool people can understand, appropriate, self-host, shape, and use without becoming dependent on an opaque institution.

A convivial communication tool gives people capacity.

It does not merely provide a service. It increases agency.

Raven should make users more capable, not more captive.

### 4.6 McLuhan Principle: The Medium Shapes the Relation

The medium is not a passive carrier. The interface changes the message.

A voice room is not just audio transport.  
A visibility indicator is not just status.  
A knock is not just a button.  
A waiting room is not just a queue.  
A mute button is not just a control.  
A role permission is not just access management.

Each one shapes expectations, norms, etiquette, anxiety, belonging, and power.

Raven must design as if every interface element teaches users how to treat each other.

### 4.7 Cybernetic Principle: Feedback Loops Become Culture

Systems create feedback loops. Feedback loops create behavior. Behavior creates culture.

If Raven rewards constant availability, users will become constantly available.  
If Raven rewards visibility, users will perform visibility.  
If Raven rewards admin control, communities will centralize power.  
If Raven rewards engagement, the product will become addictive.  
If Raven rewards clarity and consent, the culture can become more humane.

Every metric, notification, indicator, permission, and default is part of the system’s feedback structure.

Raven must design feedback loops that support trust, responsibility, and calm communication.

### 4.8 Nietzschean Principle: Do Not Imitate from Weakness

Raven should not imitate dominant products out of fear.

It should not add Discord-like text bloat because Discord has it.  
It should not add Google Calendar integration because enterprise users expect it.  
It should not add AI summaries because the market is hypnotized by AI.  
It should not add feeds because feeds create retention.  
It should not add infinite settings because power users like control.

Raven must distinguish strength from reaction.

Originality is not novelty for its own sake. Originality means knowing what the product is and having the discipline to refuse what does not belong.

## 5. Raven’s Political Position

Raven is not partisan.

But Raven is political in the deeper sense: it takes positions on power, autonomy, association, surveillance, property, institutional trust, and the rights of users inside digital spaces.

All software that mediates human communication has a political structure. The only question is whether that structure is admitted or hidden.

Raven admits it.

### 5.1 Against Platform Feudalism

Raven rejects the model where users live inside spaces they cannot inspect, cannot meaningfully leave, cannot self-host, cannot modify, cannot understand, and cannot govern.

Raven should not make users dependent on a lord of infrastructure.

Self-hosting, open-source code, exportability, transparent defaults, and honest governance are political commitments against platform feudalism.

### 5.2 Against Surveillance Capitalism and Surveillance Management

Raven rejects business models that turn human communication into behavioral inventory.

Raven also rejects enterprise models that turn employees or community members into observable productivity objects.

Surveillance does not become humane because it is sold as analytics.  
Extraction does not become ethical because it improves UX.  
Control does not become neutral because an admin requested it.

### 5.3 For User Sovereignty

Raven supports user sovereignty: the ability of users and communities to understand, control, host, configure, leave, and govern their communication environment.

Sovereignty does not mean every user gets infinite power over others.

It means users should not be powerless before the platform.

### 5.4 For Pluralism With Boundaries

Raven should allow communities to differ.

Different groups may have different norms, rituals, moderation styles, privacy expectations, and governance structures.

But pluralism is not an excuse for abuse.

Raven should refuse radical openness when openness becomes a weapon against people’s safety, dignity, or consent.

### 5.5 For the Right to Exit

A user who cannot leave is not free.

Raven should preserve exit rights through:

- Self-hostability.
- Data clarity.
- No forced central accounts.
- No artificial lock-in.
- Honest migration paths where possible.
- No punitive product design around leaving.

Raven Cloud may be convenient. It must not be captivity.

### 5.6 For Subsidiarity

Decisions should be made at the lowest competent level.

A user should control personal visibility.  
A nest should control its local access rules.  
A tree/community should define its norms.  
A self-hosted instance should govern itself.  
The Raven project should define the official product constitution.  
Raven Cloud should moderate its own hosted spaces.

Central authority should exist where necessary, not everywhere by default.

## 6. Sociological View of Raven

Raven is built for groups, not abstract users.

A communication system changes group behavior. It changes who initiates, who waits, who dominates, who hides, who feels welcome, who feels watched, who becomes responsible, and who gains informal power.

### 6.1 Small Trusted Groups First

Raven should first serve small trusted groups:

- Friends.
- Technical circles.
- Privacy-conscious groups.
- Open-source communities.
- Power users who care about communication quality and control.

Small groups are the correct starting point because Raven’s values depend on trust, context, and consent.

Large public spaces create different problems: moderation scale, discovery, spam, performative identity, harassment, and governance complexity. Raven may address these later, but they should not define the first product.

### 6.2 Presence Is Social Material

Presence is not just state. It is social material.

Showing someone as online changes what others feel allowed to ask.  
Showing someone inside a nest changes what others assume.  
Showing someone as available can create entitlement.  
Showing someone as unavailable can create respect.  
Showing too much can create pressure.  
Showing too little can kill warmth.

Raven’s presence system must therefore be built as a consent system, not merely a status system.

### 6.3 Groups Need Boundaries

Healthy groups need boundaries.

No-boundary design creates chaos.  
Over-boundary design creates bureaucracy.  
Hidden-boundary design creates mistrust.

Raven should make group boundaries understandable:

- Who can see this nest?
- Who can join?
- Who can knock?
- Who can invite?
- Who can listen?
- Who can speak?
- Who can record?
- Who can moderate?
- Who can change rules?

Boundaries should be explicit enough to create safety and light enough to preserve spontaneity.

### 6.4 Social Pressure Must Be Designed Against

Raven must assume that people can pressure each other even without intending to.

A friend asking “why didn’t you join?” can be pressure.  
A visible idle state can be pressure.  
A knock can be playful or intrusive.  
A waiting indicator can be useful or coercive.  
A persistent room can be cozy or demanding.

Raven should avoid features that make users feel socially audited.

### 6.5 Authenticity Is Not Forced Exposure

Raven values authenticity, but authenticity cannot be coerced.

A product cannot force people to “be real” by making them more visible. That is surveillance disguised as honesty.

Raven’s version of authenticity means:

- No hidden platform behavior.
- No manipulative design.
- No fake social metrics.
- No pressure to perform constant availability.
- No pretending privacy and trust are solved by branding.

Authenticity begins with the product being honest first.

## 7. Power-Dynamic Principles

Raven creates power. Therefore Raven must govern power.

Power exists in defaults, roles, visibility, admin tools, hosting, moderation, data access, UI hierarchy, notification design, and business model.

The question is never whether Raven has power. It does. The question is whether that power is visible, limited, accountable, and consent-bound.

### 7.1 Visibility Is Power

To see someone is to have power over them.

This does not make visibility bad. But it means visibility must be justified.

Raven should ask:

- Who sees this?
- Why do they see it?
- Did the user consent?
- Can the user change it?
- Is this visibility necessary?
- Can this visibility become pressure?
- Can this visibility become surveillance?

### 7.2 Defaults Are Law

Most users live inside defaults.

Therefore defaults are not neutral. They are governance.

A default can protect privacy or erode it.  
A default can reduce pressure or create it.  
A default can empower users or hide power from them.

Raven’s defaults should be conservative with exposure and generous with control.

### 7.3 Admin Power Must Be Limited

Admins need tools to protect spaces. But admin power can become surveillance power.

Raven should distinguish between:

- Legitimate moderation.
- Community governance.
- Technical administration.
- Behavioral surveillance.
- Enterprise control.

These must not be collapsed into one admin dashboard.

### 7.4 The Platform Must Not Become the Sovereign

Raven Cloud must not behave as if users exist by permission of the platform.

The platform’s legitimate role is to provide infrastructure, enforce its hosted rules, protect users, maintain reliability, and preserve the product’s constitutional commitments.

It must not become a hidden authority over private human communication.

### 7.5 Power Must Be Explainable

If Raven gives one person power over another, the product should be able to explain that power clearly.

Examples:

- “This person can join because they are a member of this tree.”
- “This admin can remove you from this nest.”
- “This host can approve knocks.”
- “This room can be seen by these roles.”
- “This recording is visible to participants.”

Hidden power destroys trust.

### 7.6 Consent Is Not a Checkbox

Consent is not a legal decoration.

Consent requires:

- Awareness.
- Specificity.
- Context.
- Reversibility where possible.
- No coercive defaults.
- No bundling of unrelated permissions.
- No deception through vague wording.

Raven should treat consent as an interaction design principle, not a settings page.

## 8. Raven’s Deepest Promise

Raven’s deepest promise is:

> **Transparency and trust.**

Raven should never do things behind the user’s back.

It should not hide how it works.  
It should not obscure what data is used.  
It should not make privacy a premium feature.  
It should not turn communication into extraction.  
It should not make people more visible than they consent to be.  
It should not pretend convenience has no cost.  
It should not pretend technology is neutral when it changes relationships.

Raven should make users feel:

> I understand what this tool does.  
> I control what I reveal.  
> I know where my communication goes.  
> I can trust this space.  
> I can leave without being trapped.  
> I am treated as a person, not a resource.

## 9. Primary Users

Raven is first for:

1. **Small trusted groups** who want a calm, reliable, low-friction place to talk.
2. **Privacy-conscious users** who want transparency, consent, and control.
3. **Power users** who care about performance, media quality, native capability, and system behavior.
4. **Open-source communities** that want trustworthy communication infrastructure.

Raven may later serve broader communities, creators, educators, and teams, but it should not begin by optimizing for enterprise organizations or mass-market social growth.

Raven should not chase everyone.

It should first deeply serve people who care how communication works.

The product should win through depth of trust before breadth of adoption.

---

## 10. Who Raven Is Not Primarily For

Raven is not primarily for:

- Enterprises seeking surveillance, compliance theater, or admin domination.
- Users who want algorithmic feeds and public performance.
- Organizations that want to measure productivity through communication metadata.
- Communities that require maximum openness at the expense of safety.
- Users who demand convenience while rejecting transparency and responsibility.
- Growth teams seeking addictive loops, dark patterns, or infinite engagement.
- People who want a single platform to absorb every form of communication.

Raven may support professional use, but it should never become an enterprise-first product at the cost of user dignity.

## 11. Product Identity

Raven should be understood as:

> **A native, open-source communication home for trusted groups, built around privacy-respecting presence, low-friction voice/video, and user control.**

The closest product archetype is:

> **The Lean Communication Home**

This archetype combines:

- The trust and autonomy of a privacy-first commons.
- The reliability and low-resource discipline of a lightweight utility.
- The warmth and accessibility of a casual communication home.
- The depth and control expected by power users.

Raven must avoid becoming:

- Open-source Discord with different branding.
- Google Meet with more settings.
- Slack huddles with privacy language.
- A protocol project with no humane app.
- A self-hosting tool only developers can tolerate.
- A surveillance-resistant product that is so difficult to use that it becomes socially irrelevant.

Raven must preserve the tension between simplicity and depth.

Its product shape should be:

> **A simple home with serious machinery underneath.**

## 12. Core Values

### 12.1 Trust

Trust is not a marketing word.

Trust is an architectural, design, governance, moderation, and business requirement.

Raven earns trust by being:

- Open-source.
- Transparent.
- Consentful.
- Self-hostable.
- Honest about trade-offs.
- Conservative with data.
- Clear about power.
- Resistant to dark patterns.

Trust cannot be demanded. It must be structurally deserved.

### 12.2 Control

Users should have control over what they reveal, how they communicate, how their media behaves, and how Raven behaves on their machine.

Control includes:

- Device control.
- Audio/video control.
- Presence control.
- Visibility control.
- Hosting control.
- Appearance control.
- Data control.
- Room access control.

Control must not become chaos.

Raven should expose power progressively, not dump complexity onto everyone.

### 12.3 Privacy

Privacy is not secrecy without responsibility.

Privacy means people are not automatically exposed, monitored, analyzed, inferred, packaged, or monetized.

Raven should protect users from:

- Hidden extraction.
- Platform surveillance.
- Corporate greed.
- Non-consensual visibility.
- Admin overreach.
- Behavioral profiling.
- AI analysis without consent.

Privacy is not a feature category. It is a condition of trust.

### 12.4 Leanness

Lean means lightweight, focused, efficient, streamlined, and bloat-free.

Every feature must justify its cost in:

- Complexity.
- Resource usage.
- Attention.
- Maintenance.
- Security.
- Cognitive load.
- UI density.
- Social consequences.

Leanness is not minimalism for aesthetics alone. It is respect for the user, the machine, the maintainer, and the social space.

### 12.5 Human Presence

Raven is for communication between humans, not content production, engagement farming, or AI-generated noise.

The product should amplify:

- Presence.
- Trust.
- Warmth.
- Responsibility.
- Mutual awareness.
- Meaningful reachability.

It should not maximize time spent. It should improve the quality of time shared.

### 12.6 Consent

Consent is required wherever Raven increases visibility, records behavior, processes personal data, enables AI features, or gives one user power over another.

Consent must be contextual.

A user may consent to be visible to one group but not another.  
A user may consent to metadata for one purpose but not another.  
A user may consent to local processing but not cloud processing.  
A user may consent to a room recording but not AI analysis.

Raven must respect these distinctions.

### 12.7 Responsibility

Raven should reject both surveillance culture and irresponsibility culture.

Users should be protected from extraction, but they should also understand that real communication has consequences.

The principle is:

> **Privacy protects dignity. It does not erase responsibility.**

## 15. Presence Philosophy

Presence is powerful and dangerous.

It can create warmth, belonging, and spontaneous connection.

It can also create pressure, judgment, obligation, and surveillance.

Raven should make presence **meaningful**, not maximally visible.

The question is not:

> How can we show everything?

The question is:

> What does this person consent to reveal, to whom, in this context, and for what purpose?

Raven should support presence states such as:

- Open to talk.
- Do not disturb.
- In a nest but not interruptible.
- Available to specific people or groups.
- Invisible or private where appropriate.
- Around without being obligated.
- Listening without being exposed, when the context allows it.

Raven should avoid:

- Exact activity trails by default.
- Pressure-heavy online indicators.
- Public visibility without context.
- Unnecessary timestamps.
- Social metrics.
- Presence history as a behavioral record.
- Visible idleness as accusation.
- Availability as entitlement.

Raven’s principle:

> **Reachability should be consentful. Presence should never become surveillance.**

## 16. Communication Philosophy

Raven is for real-time communication where human timing matters.

It should support:

- Quick calls.
- Spontaneous gatherings.
- Trusted rooms.
- Casual hanging out.
- Focused conversation.
- Shared media and screen contexts.
- Human warmth without social noise.

Raven should not become primarily about:

- Permanent chat history.
- Public content.
- Algorithmic broadcasting.
- Meeting bureaucracy.
- Workplace surveillance.
- Passive social consumption.

Raven should make it easier to ask:

> Can we talk now?

And easier to answer:

> Yes, no, later, or only under this condition.

The ability to refuse is as important as the ability to join.

A humane communication tool must design for both invitation and boundary.

## 17. Technical Philosophy

Raven’s technical philosophy is practical, native, and trust-centered.

### 17.1 Native-First

Raven should be native-first because its values depend on direct control over devices, media processing, performance, operating-system integration, overlays, audio routing, screen capture, and low-level behavior.

Browser support should not compromise Raven’s core capabilities.

Native-first is not aesthetic preference. It is philosophical alignment with control, performance, reliability, and honest device behavior.

### 17.2 Open-Source by Principle

Raven is open-source because trust requires inspectability, contribution, accountability, and independence.

Open-source is not merely a distribution strategy. It is part of Raven’s moral contract.

A communication tool that asks for trust while refusing inspection asks for faith, not trust.

### 17.3 Self-Hosting as Dignity

Self-hosting is not an advanced checkbox. It is a philosophical commitment.

Raven Cloud may provide convenience, but self-hosted Raven must never become intentionally inferior, neglected, or hostile.

Self-hosting protects the right to exit, the right to autonomy, and the right of communities to govern their own communication infrastructure.

### 17.4 Lean Architecture

Raven should have performance budgets.

Features must be judged by their cost in:

- CPU.
- Memory.
- Bandwidth.
- Startup time.
- Battery usage where relevant.
- Complexity.
- Security surface.
- Maintenance burden.
- User attention.

A feature that makes Raven impressive but heavy is suspect.

### 17.5 Reliability Before Spectacle

Communication tools fail morally when they look impressive but fail at the moment people need to talk.

Reliability, media stability, and predictable behavior come before novelty.

A failed call is not just a bug. It is a broken social promise.

### 17.6 Privacy-Aware Data Design

Raven should collect as little data as practical.

When data is needed, it must be:

- Explained.
- Consent-based.
- Purpose-limited.
- Minimized.
- Retention-limited.
- Inspectable where possible.
- Separable from unrelated permissions.

Metadata should be treated as intimate by default.

### 17.7 WebRTC Compliance, Not Browser Captivity

Raven should respect WebRTC where useful, but it should not become limited by browser-centric assumptions when native control better serves the product’s values.

The goal is not technical rebellion for its own sake. The goal is to make the communication stack serve Raven’s philosophy.

### 17.8 Extensibility Later, Discipline Now

Plugins and extensions are not MVP priorities.

Extensibility should not be used as an excuse for unclear product direction.

First, Raven must know what it is. Then it can decide what should be extensible.

### 17.9 Technical Honesty

Raven should be honest about trade-offs.

If privacy limits convenience, say so.  
If native control limits browser access, say so.  
If self-hosting adds complexity, say so.  
If E2EE limits AI summaries or moderation, say so.  
If low-resource design limits visual richness, say so.

A trustworthy product does not hide trade-offs behind marketing language.

---

## 18. AI Philosophy

Raven must not drown in AI slop.

AI may be useful later, but it must remain subordinate to human communication.

AI features must be:

- Explicit.
- Consentful.
- Visible.
- Optional.
- Explainable.
- Non-extractive.
- Easy to disable.
- Designed around user agency.

Acceptable AI directions may include:

- Consent-based summaries.
- Translation with clear indicators.
- Accessibility support.
- Noise suppression or media enhancement when locally controlled.
- User-requested catch-up features.

Dangerous AI directions include:

- Hidden listening.
- Automatic behavioral analysis.
- Social scoring.
- Unconsented meeting summaries.
- Emotion detection.
- Engagement optimization.
- AI-generated social content that pollutes human communication.
- AI agents that simulate presence in a way that confuses human relation.

Raven’s AI principle:

> **AI may assist communication, but it must never replace consent, presence, responsibility, or trust.**

AI must not become the new manager, priest, judge, therapist, spy, or ghost participant in the room.

---

## 19. Privacy and Security Philosophy

Raven should follow privacy pragmatism with strong ethical constraints.

It is not privacy absolutist at the cost of all usability, but it must never become casual about data.

Raven should treat the following as sensitive:

- Who users talk to.
- When users communicate.
- How long users stay.
- What spaces users enter.
- Presence and availability behavior.
- Device state.
- Host-machine information.
- Audio/video configuration.
- Mental, physical, occupational, or contextual signals.
- Any behavioral data that can become a profile.
- Relationship graphs.
- Repeated joining, leaving, knocking, and ignoring patterns.

Raven should assume that metadata can be intimate.

Security should not be reduced to encryption alone. Raven security includes:

- Clear permissions.
- Safe defaults.
- Transparent hosting.
- Resistance to impersonation.
- Resistance to room invasion.
- Resistance to unwanted recording.
- Resistance to stalking through presence.
- Resistance to admin overreach.

End-to-end encryption may not be required for the earliest MVP, but it must be treated as a serious architectural destination, not an afterthought.

---

## 20. Moderation Philosophy

Raven rejects both platform surveillance and naive openness.

Moderation should protect people from harm without normalizing hidden monitoring.

Acceptable moderation tools include:

- Role permissions.
- Kick and ban.
- Room-level controls.
- Community-defined rules.
- Clear recording indicators.
- Server-level federation or instance blocking if federation exists later.
- Cloud moderation for Raven-hosted public/shared spaces.

Dangerous moderation tools include:

- Hidden audio/video monitoring.
- Behavioral scoring.
- Enterprise dashboards that expose user behavior.
- AI surveillance without explicit consent.
- Admin access to private communication by default.
- Productivity-style activity analytics.

Self-hosted instances are responsible for their own governance, but Raven should still define what official Raven values are.

Raven should distinguish between:

- The software as a tool.
- Raven Cloud as a hosted service.
- Individual self-hosted instances as autonomous spaces.
- The Raven project as the steward of official principles.

These layers should not be morally or operationally confused.

---

## 21. Governance Philosophy

Raven should be open-source, but not directionless.

The project should have:

- A clear constitution.
- Strong founder/core-team direction early on.
- A maintainer council as the project matures.
- Public reasoning for major decisions.
- Community input on implementation, usability, sustainability, and data practices.
- Non-negotiable principles around privacy, anti-surveillance, anti-dark-pattern design, and anti-bloat discipline.

Raven should not allow public vote to override its moral foundations.

Open governance does not mean every principle is negotiable. It means decisions are made transparently, with accountability, and with respect for contributors and users.

Long-term, Raven may belong to a foundation, but only after its constitution is strong enough to survive institutional drift.

### 21.1 Constitutional Governance

Raven should be governed by constitutional principles, not by impulse.

The constitution defines what cannot be traded away:

- Open-source core.
- Self-hosting respect.
- No data sales.
- No dark patterns.
- No privacy upsells.
- No enterprise surveillance.
- No hidden AI listening.
- No bloat as strategy.

Community participation can shape the product. It cannot nullify the product’s moral commitments.

### 21.2 Contributor Culture

Raven should cultivate contributors who value:

- Technical excellence.
- Clarity.
- Restraint.
- User dignity.
- Performance.
- Honest disagreement.
- Maintenance discipline.
- Respect for the product constitution.

Raven should reject contributor behavior that normalizes harassment, sabotage, ideological capture, entitlement, opaque decision-making, or careless expansion of scope.

## 22. Business Philosophy

Raven may make money.

Raven must not become extractive.

Acceptable models:

- Donations.
- Paid hosted Raven Cloud.
- Paid support.
- Ethical managed deployments.
- Grants.
- Possibly paid convenience features, if they do not compromise dignity or privacy.

Forbidden models:

- Selling user data.
- Ads.
- Selling privacy features.
- Dark patterns.
- Forced central accounts.
- Hostile self-hosting.
- Enterprise surveillance features.
- Growth at all costs.
- Lock-in as strategy.

Raven may charge for convenience.  
Raven must not charge for dignity.

Raven may charge for hosting.  
Raven must not punish self-hosting.

Raven may be commercially sustainable.  
Raven must not be commercially obedient.

### 22.1 The Ethical Monetization Rule

A paid feature is acceptable only if it does not create a dignity hierarchy.

It is acceptable to charge for:

- Hosting.
- Storage.
- Bandwidth-heavy convenience.
- Managed infrastructure.
- Support.
- Administrative convenience that does not become surveillance.

It is not acceptable to charge for:

- Basic privacy.
- Basic security.
- Freedom from ads.
- Freedom from tracking.
- Data export.
- Self-hosting viability.
- Transparency.

A user should never feel that Raven protects rich users better than poor users.

## 25. Product Decision Filter

Every major feature should pass these questions:

1. Does this help people communicate more meaningfully?
2. Does this preserve or improve user control?
3. Does this reduce trust, privacy, or transparency?
4. Does this add bloat?
5. Does this create social pressure?
6. Does this require hidden data collection?
7. Does this make self-hosting harder?
8. Does this serve Raven’s users or Raven’s growth metrics?
9. Can this be explained honestly in one sentence?
10. Would we still build this if growth were not a goal?
11. Who gains power because of this feature?
12. Who becomes more visible because of this feature?
13. Who carries the cognitive cost of this feature?
14. Can a user refuse this feature without punishment?
15. Does this feature make Raven more human, or merely more impressive?

If a feature fails the trust, privacy, power, or bloat test, it should be rejected or redesigned.

---

## 26. Power Review Filter

Every feature that changes visibility, moderation, identity, data, roles, AI, or admin control must answer:

1. What power does this create?
2. Who receives that power?
3. Who becomes subject to that power?
4. Is the power visible?
5. Is the power limited?
6. Is the power reversible?
7. Is consent required?
8. Can the affected user understand what is happening?
9. Can this become surveillance?
10. Can this become social pressure?
11. Can this be abused by admins, hosts, friends, communities, or Raven Cloud?
12. What is the safest default?

No power-changing feature should ship without this review.

## 30. Core Tensions Raven Must Keep Alive

Raven should not pretend its tensions are solved.

It must actively manage them.

### 30.1 Simplicity vs Control

Raven must be simple enough for trusted groups and deep enough for power users.

The answer is progressive disclosure, not false simplicity or exposed chaos.

### 30.2 Privacy vs Accountability

Raven must protect people from surveillance while preserving social responsibility.

The answer is consentful boundaries, not hidden monitoring.

### 30.3 Self-Hosting vs Hosted Convenience

Raven Cloud may be easier, but self-hosted Raven must remain respected.

The answer is convenience without captivity.

### 30.4 Governance Openness vs Product Direction

Raven should welcome contribution without becoming directionless.

The answer is constitutional openness, not pure democracy or closed dictatorship.

### 30.5 Sustainability vs Moral Compromise

Raven needs money to survive, but survival is not worth every price.

The answer is ethical monetization, not purity theater or extraction.

### 30.6 Presence vs Pressure

Raven needs presence to create warmth, but too much presence becomes pressure.

The answer is meaningful reachability, not maximal visibility.

## 32. Final Statement

Raven exists because communication tools should not be bloated, opaque, extractive, or hostile to human dignity.

Raven is for people who care about communication, control, reliability, privacy, and trust.

Raven refuses to do anything important without users knowing what is happening.

Raven treats users as human beings, not data sources.

Raven understands that software is never just software: it is a social structure, a power arrangement, a set of defaults, and a philosophy made executable.

Raven’s strongest enemy is corporate greed.

Raven’s deeper enemy is the belief that human presence must be captured, measured, optimized, and monetized to be valuable.

Raven’s deepest promise is transparency and trust.

Raven is a lean communication home for humans.
