# Raven Design Philosophy Manifesto

**Version:** 0.1  
**Purpose:** UI/UX, interaction, onboarding, visual language, product surface, and feature-design principles for Raven.

---

## 1. Design Thesis

Raven should feel like a **quiet communication home**: calm, fast, native, warm, trustworthy, and under the user’s control.

The interface should not feel like a corporate dashboard, a social feed, a gamer control panel, a productivity tool, or an enterprise admin system.

Raven’s design should make three things immediately clear:

1. **Who is meaningfully reachable?**
2. **What spaces are alive right now?**
3. **What control do I have before I enter?**

Everything else is secondary.

---

## 2. Design North Star

The design north star is:

> **Simple surface, deep control.**

Raven should be understandable to casual users, but satisfying to power users.

The default experience should be calm and obvious. Advanced controls should exist, but they should not dominate the product surface.

Raven should never punish users for not being technical.  
Raven should never betray power users by hiding meaningful control forever.

---

## 3. What Raven Should Feel Like

Raven should feel:

- Lean.
- Native.
- Trustworthy.
- Calm.
- Warm.
- Direct.
- Fast.
- Human.
- Focused.
- Respectful.
- Slightly cyberpunk, but not theatrical.
- Powerful underneath, quiet on the surface.

Raven should not feel:

- Bloated.
- Corporate.
- Gamified by default.
- Juvenile.
- Surveillance-heavy.
- Dashboard-like.
- Algorithmic.
- Over-socialized.
- Enterprise-first.
- AI-generated.
- Visually noisy.

---

## 4. Core UX Principles

### 4.1 The user owns their signal

The mic, camera, audio routing, screen sharing, mixer, quality, and processing controls are not secondary settings. They represent the user’s agency.

Device controls should feel persistent, trustworthy, and always reachable.

The user should never wonder:

- Am I muted?
- Is my camera on?
- What audio is being shared?
- Who can hear me?
- Who can see me?
- What am I about to reveal?

### 4.2 Presence must be consentful

Presence is not a universal broadcast.

The UI should reveal presence only in ways that respect context, role, relationship, and user intent.

Avoid designs that make users feel watched, judged, or socially cornered.

### 4.3 Friction should protect, not obstruct

Good friction creates consent, clarity, privacy, or safety.

Bad friction creates confusion, delay, annoyance, or unnecessary decision-making.

### 4.4 Power should be layered

Do not flatten Raven into either extreme:

- Too simple: users lose meaningful control.
- Too powerful: users drown in knobs.

Use progressive disclosure:

- Default controls for everyone.
- Advanced controls for people who seek them.
- Expert diagnostics for people who need them.

### 4.5 The interface should not beg for attention

Raven should not compete with social feeds. It should not create artificial urgency.

The UI should be alive when people are alive, but quiet when nothing meaningful is happening.

### 4.6 Make state obvious

Every important state should be visible without anxiety:

- Am I connected?
- Am I audible?
- Am I visible?
- Who is here?
- Who can join?
- Who can knock?
- What permissions apply?
- Is this space private?
- Is recording or AI active?

### 4.7 Do not design for engagement

Raven should not optimize for time spent, streaks, dopamine loops, social comparison, infinite notifications, or habitual checking.

Design for successful communication, not retention addiction.

---

## 5. Information Architecture

Raven’s main structure should be organized around:

1. **People**
2. **Trees**
3. **Nests**
4. **Current device/control state**
5. **Pending invitations, knocks, or calls**

The product should not be organized around feeds, channels, metrics, dashboards, or content discovery.

---

## 6. The Main Areas

### 6.1 Left sidebar

The left sidebar is the live social map.

It should answer:

- Who is active?
- Which trees have live activity?
- Which nests are currently alive?
- Who wants my attention?
- Where can I quickly join?

It should not show everything. It should show what is meaningful now.

Principles:

- Prefer active/relevant items over exhaustive structure.
- Group activity clearly.
- Avoid making categories look like tree names.
- Avoid noisy unread-style pressure unless necessary.
- Show enough context to act, not enough to surveil.

### 6.2 Main container

The main container is contextual.

It should change based on what the user selects:

- Launch Home.
- Tree Lobby.
- User Page.
- Temporary Nest Waiting Room.
- Active Nest.

The main container should not become generic empty space. It should always answer:

> What is this place, what is happening here, and what can I safely do?

### 6.3 Bottom control bar

The bottom mic/cam/mixer/share control bar should remain persistent because it embodies Raven’s philosophy:

> My device. My signal. My control.

It should be available regardless of whether the user is inside a nest.

This avoids repeatedly forcing users through pre-call setup and reinforces the idea that readiness belongs to the user, not the room.

---

## 7. Screen Philosophy

### 7.1 Launch Home

The launch home should feel like entering a quiet communication home.

It should show:

- Your current device readiness.
- Active people you care about.
- Active nests in your trees.
- Pending knocks, invites, or calls.
- Quick start/join actions.
- Recent spaces only if useful.

It should not show:

- A social feed.
- Analytics.
- Historical surveillance trails.
- Productivity widgets.
- AI-generated summaries by default.
- Gamified activity metrics.

The launch home’s primary question:

> Who is meaningfully reachable right now?

### 7.2 Tree Lobby

A tree lobby is the entry point into a trusted group.

It should show:

- Active nests.
- Who is inside, if visibility allows.
- Join, knock, or request actions.
- Nest purpose and access rules.
- Tree-level context.
- Relevant people.

It should avoid overwhelming users with every inactive nest unless the user explicitly wants structure management.

The tree lobby’s primary question:

> What is alive in this group right now?

### 7.3 User Page

A user page is not a profile for performance. It is a relationship-control surface.

It should show:

- Current reachability.
- Call/knock/invite options.
- Shared trees or nests.
- Mutual visibility/permission state.
- Relationship-specific controls.

It should not show creepy history, behavior trails, or social metrics.

The user page’s primary question:

> How can I respectfully reach this person?

### 7.4 Temporary Nest Waiting Room

The waiting room should not be a corporate pre-call clone.

It should show:

- What you are joining.
- Who invited you or who is inside.
- Your mic/cam/share state.
- Privacy implications.
- Join, decline, or ask options.

The waiting room’s primary question:

> Am I ready, and do I consent to enter under these conditions?

### 7.5 Active Nest

An active nest should make communication feel direct and stable.

It should show:

- Participants.
- Speaking state.
- Mute/deafen/camera/share state.
- Role or permission context when relevant.
- Clear leave controls.
- Recording/AI/privacy indicators if active.

It should not become overloaded with chat, feeds, widgets, or analytics unless those features are explicitly part of the nest’s purpose.

The active nest’s primary question:

> Who is here, what is shared, and what am I revealing?

---

## 8. Presence Design

Presence should be designed as a permissioned language, not a broadcast system.

### 8.1 Good presence states

Useful presence states include:

- Open to talk.
- Do not disturb.
- In a nest but not interruptible.
- Around.
- Focused.
- Invisible/private.

Presence states should be simple, human-readable, and explainable.

### 8.2 Dangerous presence patterns

Avoid:

- Exact online duration.
- Passive listening trails.
- “Seen” pressure where unnecessary.
- Public interruption signals.
- Too many micro-statuses.
- Presence history by default.
- Making absence socially suspicious.

### 8.3 Presence rule

> Show enough to enable respectful contact. Hide enough to prevent social pressure.

---

## 9. Knock and Join Philosophy

Knocking is valuable because it creates a softer alternative to barging in or sending a formal invite.

A good knock should feel:

- Light.
- Respectful.
- Dismissible.
- Contextual.
- Non-demanding.

Knocking should not become spam, pressure, or performance.

Rules:

- Knocks should be rate-limited or socially bounded.
- Users should be able to mute knocks.
- Nests should define who can knock, join, or see occupancy.
- Declining a knock should not feel rude.
- Ignoring a knock should be acceptable.

The knock principle:

> A knock asks for presence without claiming entitlement to it.

---

## 10. Visual Language

Raven’s visual design should support trust, calm, and control.

### 10.1 Aesthetic direction

Recommended direction:

- Dark or neutral native desktop feel.
- Minimal but warm surfaces.
- Clear hierarchy.
- Soft depth, not decorative clutter.
- Glass or transparency only where it improves spatial understanding.
- Motion used sparingly for state changes, not spectacle.
- Cyberpunk hints through precision, contrast, and technical elegance, not neon chaos.

### 10.2 Avoid

Avoid:

- Excessive gradients.
- Social-media visual noise.
- Enterprise dashboard density.
- Gamer-brand aggression.
- Overly cute mascot energy.
- AI-generated illustration slop.
- Decorative complexity without function.

### 10.3 Visual principle

> Every color, shade, border, blur, radius, animation, and icon should have a reason.

---

## 11. Interaction Design

### 11.1 Joining

Joining should be fast, but never ambiguous.

Before joining, users should know:

- What space they are entering.
- Whether they will be visible.
- Whether they will be audible.
- Whether camera/screen sharing is active.
- Whether recording or AI is active.
- Whether joining alerts others.

### 11.2 Leaving

Leaving should be calm and immediate.

Avoid guilt-heavy UX. Do not make leaving socially dramatic by default.

### 11.3 Muting and device control

Mute/camera/share states must be impossible to misunderstand.

Use redundant cues where needed:

- Icon.
- Label.
- Color/state.
- Tooltip.
- Optional sound/haptic feedback where appropriate.

### 11.4 Advanced controls

Advanced media controls should be discoverable but not front-and-center.

Examples:

- Bitrate.
- Resolution.
- FPS.
- Audio processing.
- Noise suppression.
- Routing.
- Mixer controls.
- Diagnostics.

Default users should not need to understand these. Power users should not feel patronized.

---

## 12. Onboarding Philosophy

Onboarding should teach Raven’s mental model, not just buttons.

Users need to understand:

- What trees are.
- What nests are.
- What presence means.
- What knocking means.
- How privacy works.
- How device control works.
- What self-hosting/cloud means if relevant.

Onboarding should be short, contextual, and skippable.

Do not explain everything upfront. Teach concepts at the moment they matter.

### 12.1 First-run priorities

First-run onboarding should answer:

1. What is Raven?
2. How do I control mic/cam/audio?
3. How do I join or create a nest?
4. Who can see me?
5. What does Raven not do with my data?

### 12.2 Avoid

Avoid:

- Long tutorial flows.
- Corporate welcome screens.
- Empty marketing claims.
- Forced account complexity.
- Permission requests without explanation.

---

## 13. Copywriting and Tone

Raven’s copy should be:

- Clear.
- Honest.
- Human.
- Calm.
- Direct.
- Non-corporate.
- Non-manipulative.

Avoid:

- “Boost engagement.”
- “Stay connected all the time.”
- “Never miss anything.”
- “Your team’s productivity hub.”
- “AI-powered everything.”
- Fake friendliness.
- Dark-pattern urgency.

Prefer:

- “Open to talk.”
- “Ask to join.”
- “You are not visible here.”
- “Your microphone is muted.”
- “This nest allows knocks from friends.”
- “Recording requires consent.”
- “Raven Cloud is optional. Self-hosting is supported.”

Tone principle:

> Be warm, but never manipulative. Be clear, but never cold.

---

## 14. Privacy UX

Privacy should not live only in settings.

Privacy should be visible at the moment of risk.

Show privacy cues when:

- Joining a nest.
- Becoming visible.
- Turning on mic/camera.
- Sharing screen/system audio.
- Recording starts.
- AI features activate.
- Admin/community rules affect visibility.
- Data leaves the device or instance.

Privacy UX should explain consequences without overwhelming users.

Bad privacy UX says:

> Accept all.

Good privacy UX says:

> Here is what changes if you continue.

---

## 15. Accessibility and Cognitive Load

Raven should be accessible by default.

Accessibility is part of leanness because inaccessible design creates hidden friction.

Principles:

- Strong contrast.
- Clear focus states.
- Keyboard navigation.
- Screen-reader-friendly labels.
- Reduced motion support.
- Clear icons with labels where needed.
- Avoid reliance on color alone.
- Avoid dense controls without grouping.
- Support low-end devices.
- Support weak networks gracefully.

Cognitive load principle:

> The user should spend attention on people, not on decoding the interface.

---

## 16. Notifications

Notifications should be rare, meaningful, and controllable.

Raven should reject infinite notification culture.

Notify for:

- Direct calls.
- Knocks.
- Invites.
- Important permission or privacy changes.
- Critical connection/device issues.

Avoid notifying for:

- Generic activity.
- Engagement nudges.
- “Someone is online” spam.
- Retention reminders.
- Social comparison.
- Algorithmic recommendations.

Notification principle:

> A notification should respect attention as a finite human resource.

---

## 17. Error and Failure Design

Communication failure is emotionally costly. Raven should handle failure calmly and specifically.

Bad error:

> Something went wrong.

Good error:

> Raven cannot access your microphone because the operating system denied permission.

Errors should explain:

- What happened.
- Why it likely happened.
- What the user can do.
- Whether privacy or visibility was affected.

Failure states should never create uncertainty about whether the user is audible or visible.

---

## 18. Design Anti-Patterns

Reject:

- Main screens full of empty decorative cards.
- Dashboards that measure communication behavior.
- Presence systems that create social anxiety.
- Modals for every action.
- Settings sprawl.
- “Advanced” controls mixed into default flows.
- Hidden recording/AI states.
- Enterprise-style admin panels in the main product.
- Algorithmic feeds.
- Public popularity metrics.
- Gamification that pressures users to stay.
- AI-generated summaries by default.
- Forced onboarding tours.
- Unclear room/nest permissions.
- Ambiguous mic/camera state.

---

## 19. Feature Design Filter

Before adding a feature, ask:

1. Does this improve real communication?
2. Does it preserve calm?
3. Does it increase control without creating clutter?
4. Does it reduce or increase social pressure?
5. Does it require new sensitive data?
6. Does it make self-hosting harder?
7. Does it harm native performance?
8. Does it belong on the surface or in the basement?
9. Would this still matter if Raven had no growth targets?
10. Is this feature humane, or merely impressive?

If a feature is technically impressive but spiritually wrong, cut it.

---

## 20. UI Hierarchy Rule

Raven’s UI hierarchy should follow this order:

1. Current user safety and device state.
2. Active human presence.
3. Available communication actions.
4. Space context and permissions.
5. Advanced controls.
6. History, diagnostics, and configuration.

Do not invert this order.

Never put metrics above humans.  
Never put customization above clarity.  
Never put growth above trust.

---

## 21. Layout Implications

### 21.1 Persistent bottom controls

Keep mic, camera, mixer, deafen, and sharing controls fixed at the bottom.

Reason:

- They belong to the user, not to a specific nest.
- They reduce repeated pre-call friction.
- They reinforce user agency.
- They make readiness continuous.

### 21.2 Main container should be contextual

Do not treat the main area as generic blank canvas.

Each selected object should produce a meaningful layout:

- Home: meaningful reachability.
- Tree: live group activity.
- User: respectful contact surface.
- Temp nest: consentful entry.
- Active nest: clear communication state.

### 21.3 Sidebar should show live relevance

The sidebar should prioritize active and actionable things.

It should not become a full structural tree explorer unless the user enters management mode.

---

## 22. MVP Design Boundaries

The MVP should design deeply for:

- Native desktop use.
- Friend groups and trusted small communities.
- Voice/video-first interaction.
- Lean presence.
- Clear device control.
- Role-based nests.
- Knocking/joining flows.
- Self-hosting trust cues.
- Privacy visibility.
- Reliable low-resource operation.

The MVP should not design deeply for:

- Mobile-first workflows.
- Enterprise dashboards.
- Large public discovery.
- Plugin marketplaces.
- AI meeting assistants.
- Permanent chat ecosystems.
- Complex federation UX.
- Creator monetization.
- Education workflows.
- Corporate scheduling systems.

---

## 23. The Raven Interface Test

A Raven screen is good if it helps users answer:

- Where am I?
- Who is here?
- Who can reach me?
- Who can see me?
- What am I sharing?
- What can I safely do next?
- How do I leave?
- How do I change my level of visibility?

A Raven screen is bad if it makes users wonder:

- Am I being watched?
- Am I audible?
- Why is this here?
- What does this icon mean?
- Why am I being pushed to engage?
- Why is this so heavy?
- Why do I need this feature?
- Who benefits from this design?

---

## 24. Final Design Statement

Raven’s design should make communication feel lighter, clearer, safer, and more human.

It should give casual users a calm surface and power users real depth.

It should make privacy visible without making privacy exhausting.

It should make presence useful without making presence oppressive.

It should make control available without making control obsessive.

It should make Raven feel like a place people can trust, not a system trying to capture them.

Raven’s interface should be lean because attention is precious.  
Raven’s controls should be clear because communication is vulnerable.  
Raven’s presence should be consentful because humans are not availability indicators.  
Raven’s design should serve people, not growth.
